Abstract  
This report presents the third version of the Global map of Forest Cover for the year 2020 at 10 m 
spatial resolution (GFC2020 V3, dated 28 November 2025) and the first version of the Global map 
of Forest Types for the year 2020 (GFT2020 V1, dated 15 January 2026). These maps are based on 
the combination of several global land cover / land use data sets and provide spatially explicit 
representations of forest presence and absence (GFC2020) and main forest types (GFT2020). The 
report presents input datasets, mapping methodology and a comparison with other global sources 
of information. For GFC2020 we also present an accuracy assessment of this new map version and 
a comparison with the previous version. Results show an improved accuracy and very good match 
with the global forest area estimate for year 2020 of the latest report of the Global Forest 
Resources Assessment by the Food and Agriculture Organization. GFC2020 and GFT2020 can 
support the implementation of the EU Regulation on Deforestation-free supply chains (Regulation 
(EU) 2023/1115), notably in the phase of risk assessment, but their use is not mandatory, not 
exclusive and not legally binding. The primary access to this map is via the EU Observatory on 
Deforestation and Forest Degradation.

Foreword 
Forests are indispensable to life on Earth, delivering critical ecosystem services by producing 
oxygen, sequestering carbon, regulating climate and water cycles, filtering air and water, and 
sustaining vast biodiversity. They underpin the livelihoods of around 1.6 billion people, provide raw 
materials for a growing bioeconomy, and carry profound social, cultural, and spiritual significance, 
particularly for indigenous communities.  
Despite their importance, forests are under increasing pressure: according to the FAO Global Forest 
Resources Assessment 2025, deforestation continues at an alarming rate while ongoing 
degradation further diminishes ecosystem functionality, biodiversity, and carbon storage. 
In response, the European Union has advanced a key initiative for sustainable competitiveness 
through the Regulation on deforestation-free products (EUDR). This regulation requires that 
operators placing relevant commodities (cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rubber, soya, and wood) and 
their derived products on the EU market, or exporting them from it, demonstrate that they were not 
produced on land subject to deforestation or forest degradation after 2020. Compliance entails, 
among other requirements, providing precise geolocation data of sourcing areas and verifying their 
legality. Although not explicitly mandated by the EUDR, maps play a key role in supporting 
deforestation and forest degradation risk assessments within due diligence processes. 
To this end, the Joint Research Centre (JRC) has developed the Global Forest Cover 2020 (GFC2020) 
and Global Forest Type 2020 (GFT2020) datasets – harmonized, globally consistent maps of forest 
presence and main forest types at 10-meter spatial resolution. This report presents the 
methodology and results of the third version of GFC2020 and the first version of GFT2020, and 
highlights key areas for future improvement. Even though these maps are not legally binding or 
mandatory under the EUDR, they are intended to support operators and traders in conducting 
spatially explicit risk assessments, potentially in combination with other datasets, to help 
demonstrate compliance with deforestation-free requirements.

Executive summary 
Policy context 
The EU Regulation on deforestation-free supply chains (EUDR; Regulation (EU) 2023/1115) sets out 
rules and obligations for a set of commodities (cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber, soya, wood) 
and derived products to reduce global deforestation and forest degradation induced by the EU. 
Deforestation-free means that commodities and products do not originate from land that was 
subject to deforestation or forest degradation after 31 December 2020. Operators and traders 
putting or making available above-mentioned commodities and derived products on the EU market, 
or exporting from it, must exercise due diligence by providing information and data, including the 
geolocation of the sourcing areas. Spatial information about the land use in 2020 could be highly 
beneficial to support the risk assessment of the EUDR.  
Key conclusions 
In this context, the Joint Research Centre (JRC) developed the Global Forest Cover map for the year 
2020 (GFC2020) and the Global Forest Type map for the year 2020 (GFT2020) at 10 m spatial 
resolution. The EU Observatory on Deforestation and Forest Degradation (EUFO) is the primary 
access to both maps and other sources of global forest maps, spatial forest and forestry-related 
information, global land use carbon fluxes, and scientific data on supply chains.  
GFC2020 and GFT2020 are support tools offered free of charge by the European Commission to 
operators and traders for the implementation of the EUDR deforestation risk assessment. However, 
GFC2020 and GFT2020, like any other global, regional, or national map, are non-mandatory, non-
exclusive, and non-legally binding sources of information. The colocation of geolocation data (points 
or polygons) with forest areas in GFC2020, specific forest types in GFT2020, or forest-related 
indicators in other geospatial sources, does not automatically indicate non-compliance with the 
EUDR and vice versa. Instead, such plots should undergo further assessment to determine the 
actual risk of deforestation or forest degradation with more detailed or locally relevant data for a 
robust risk evaluation.  
Main findings 
Based on updated and new input data, the third version (V3) of GFC2020 improved in terms of 
forest area estimates and map accuracy. The new Copernicus global land cover map and updated 
forest loss drivers and forest management layers helped detecting more potential forest areas, 
including unstocked forest land, whereas commodity-specific data sets enabled targeted 
improvements to exclude tree crops and areas under shifting cultivation or agroforestry systems 
(see infographic below).  
 

 
7 
The overall accuracy of GFC2020 V3 improved to 92.8% (GFC2020 V2: 91.5%) with an omission 
error of 10.6% and a commission error of 13.1% for forest. Moreover, there is a close match 
between the global forest area derived from GFC2020 and the forest area estimate for the year 
2020 from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Global Forest Resources Assessment (FRA) 
published in 2025. Even though the map improved in critical areas there are still regions where the 
distinction between forest and non-forest is challenging. The product could benefit further from 
refined data layers with a higher spatial resolution than as they are available today.  
The report also documents the mapping approach for the first version of GFT2020. Based on the 
forest area extent mapped in GFC2020 V3, specific processes identify three forest types: (i) primary 
forest, (ii) naturally regenerating forest and (iii) planted forest, including plantation forests. This 
mapping is mostly based on indirect indicators or proxies, e.g. distance to artificial land features 
such as roads or the protection status to infer primary forests. The comparison to other sources 
shows some differences that can be related to uncertainties (on both sides) and the general 
difficulty to characterize forest types by remote sensing. No formal accuracy assessment for the 
three main forest types is yet available. The GFC2020 V3 accuracy assessment remains applicable 
to the aggregated forest cover across all three forest types, but does not evaluate the accuracy or 
omission/commission errors of the forest type disaggregation in GFT2020 V1. 
Related and future Joint Research Centre work 
This report builds on previous work, including the first two versions of GFC2020 and a preliminary 
version of GFT2020 (Bourgoin et al., 2024a, 2025, 2026), the development of an accuracy 
assessment framework for validating GFC2020 (Colditz et al. 2025a) and insights gained from 
geospatial assessment tool development (Simonetti et al., 2025) and regional studies (Khiripet et al. 
2026, Verhegghen et al. 2024). The JRC plans to release a fourth version of GFC2020, a second 
version of GFT2020 and accuracy assessments for both products by the end of 2026. The JRC also 
collaborates with European Commission services to ensure interoperability between the EUDR 
Information System and GFC2020. 
Quick guide 
This report describes the input data and methodology to produce GFC2020 V3 and GFT2020 V1. 
The result section illustrates the mapping result and compares these maps against other global 
data sets. The accuracy assessment of GFC2020 V3 is described in a dedicated section of the 
report.

1. Introduction  
The Regulation “on the making available on the Union market as well as export from the Union of 
certain commodities and products associated with deforestation and forest degradation” (EU) 
2023/1115), in the following abbreviated “EUDR”, went into force in June 20231. This Regulation 
lays down rules and obligations for placing seven commodities (cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, 
rubber, soya, wood) and associated products on the EU market or exporting from it. Commodities 
and products must be deforestation-free, produced legally according to the rules of the country of 
origin, and covered by a due diligence statement. Operators need to exercise due diligence, by 
providing information, including geographic coordinates about the sourcing location, and under 
certain conditions a risk assessment and risk mitigation.  
Deforestation-free means that commodities and products are not sourced from land that 
underwent deforestation or forest degradation after the cut-off date (31 December 2020). 
Deforestation refers to the conversion of land from forest to agricultural land use. Forest 
degradation refers to (i) a conversion of primary forests or naturally regenerating forests to 
plantation forests or other wooded land or (ii) a conversion of primary forest to planted forest (see 
also annex 1). 
The obligation to provide the geolocation as point or polygon if the area is larger than 4 ha for 
commodities and products makes maps for the year 2020 a useful tool to assess the risk of 
deforestation or forest degradation. To this end the JRC developed maps of Global Forest Cover for 
the year 2020 (GFC2020) and Global Forest Types for the year 2020 (GFT2020), notably to support 
the risk assessment for deforestation and forest degradation, respectively. Adhering to the forest 
definition as set out in the EUDR, GFC2020 maps forest cover presence and absence at 10m spatial 
resolution in the year 2020 from a wealth of existing data layers. Previous work describes the 
mapping of GFC2020 at version 1 (Bourgoin et al., 2024a, Colditz et al., 2024) and version 2 
(Bourgoin et al., 2025, 2026), including a dedicated map assessment (Colditz et al., 2025a). Based 
on the forest extent of GFC2020, GFT2020 maps three forest types: primary forest, naturally 
regenerating forest and planted forest which also includes plantation forest. Given the definition of 
forest degradation, it is not critical to distinguish between planted forest and plantation forest in 
2020. This distinction is only necessary for information on the state of land post-2020, which is 
provided via the geolocation (see also annex 1). As the mapping of forest types is based on fewer 
data layers and builds largely in secondary information and proxies, the JRC released a preliminary 
map (GFT2020 version 0) in 2025 (Bourgoin et al., 2025) with the aim to collect feedback.

The EU Observatory on Deforestation and Forest Degradation (EUFO) hosts GFC2020 and GFT2020 
maps for viewing. All maps can be accessed and downloaded free-of-charge via various portals 
(see section 3.1). GFC2020 and GFT2020 are support tools provided by the European Commission 
with no authoritative status; hence their use is non-mandatory, non-exclusive and not legally 
binding. The latter means that a spatial match or non-match between a geolocation in the due 
diligence statement and forest or a specific forest type in the maps does neither mean with full 
confidence that the parcel has been deforested/degraded or not been deforested/degraded since 
2020. In any case, we recommend to pool different spatial and non-spatial data sources for risk 
assessment. These maps may also be used for verification by competent authorities, e.g. when 
selecting sample areas to carry out more detailed and robust checks.  
The main objective of this report is to present the input data and the approach for generating the 
third version (V3) of GFC2020 and the first version (V1) of GFT2020. The report presents also an 
analysis of the resulting maps in comparison with other global or international sources. An accuracy 
assessment has been carried out for GFC2020 V3 and is described in a dedicated section in this 
report.

2. Data and method 
The approach for identifying forest area and forest types combines existing global wall-to-wall 
datasets or datasets that are global for their scope. “Global for their scope” means that wall-to-wall 
data layers for zones or ecoregions are used if they provide a global coverage, e.g. for all tropical 
forests or dry forests on all continents. On the contrary, the workflow does not incorporate data at 
local, regional national or continental level. The maps are therefore a harmonized, globally 
consistent representation for the presence or absence of forest cover and forest types in 2020, at 
10m spatial resolution. 
2.1. Update of the mapping approach for GFC2020 version 3 
2.1.1. Input datasets 
Translating the EUDR’s forest cover definition into a binary forest/non-forest map presents a 
significant challenge. Not every tree-covered pixel corresponds to a forest, and not all forested 
areas contain tree cover. Converting tree cover data into “forest” land use requires information on 
tree height, canopy density, and area, as well as supplementary contextual data, because some 
treed areas may not meet the criteria for forests or may serve other purposes, such as agriculture 
or urban development. Similarly, forests can temporarily lose tree cover due to disturbances like 
fires or logging, yet still qualify as forest land use, with the potential for trees to recover that will 
meet the required thresholds for height and canopy density. 
GFC2020 V3 integrates new or updated datasets released since the publication of GFC2020 V2 and 
incorporates user feedback to enhance the accuracy and comprehensiveness of the global forest 
extent. This update corrects overestimations in agricultural tree plantation areas, mixed-use 
landscapes (e.g., shifting cultivation and agroforestry systems), as well as underestimations of 
planted forests and unstocked areas. 
This section describes the updated input layers for land cover, land use, tree cover, and tree height 
used to produce the GFC2020 V3 map (Table 1). Most datasets represent conditions around 2020, 
with exceptions noted and justified in the following sections. Many datasets provide thematic 
classifications. In this new version, we increased the use of datasets offering continuous probability 
values, which require tailored thresholding within the workflow. The integration also uses 
stratification layers, including global ecological zones, tree cover change drivers, forest 
management types, and coarse cropland production maps, to refine and enhance the mapping 
process.

2.1.2. Approach 
The approach for mapping forest areas in year 2020 at 10m resolution using the EUDR definition of 
forests includes three steps:  
• Step 1: mapping the maximum potential extent of forest cover for year 2020 globally. This 
includes areas of currently unstocked forest land from harvesting operations, fire (when its 
direct cause is natural) and other natural disturbance (e.g., storms, flooding, landslides, 
drought, windthrow, lava flows, sediment flow or meandering rivers, natural flooding, insect 
outbreaks). This step is crucial, as areas not included in the maximum potential forest cover 
extent cannot be classified as forest cover in subsequent steps. It also includes an 
intermediate step that maps high-confidence natural and planted forests to override the 
exclusion masks applied in Step 2. 
• Step 2: apply overlays and Boolean decision rules to reduce the maximum potential forest 
cover extent to the EUDR forest definition using global datasets that cover tree height, 
agricultural use (i.e. deforestation, cropland, tree crops, shifting agriculture and 
agroforestry) and other land uses (i.e. non-forest ecosystems and urban). It also includes 
minor corrections to the output layer (e.g. manual removal striping artefacts).  
• Step 3: apply a Minimum Mapping Unit (MMU) of 0.5 ha to forest and non-forest patches. 
Figure 1 illustrates the steps and decision rules in detail. The selection and integration of input 
datasets are designed to balance commission and omission errors of forest cover globally. However, 
this tendency may vary regionally and may even be reversed in intensively managed or man-made 
forest landscapes.

2.1.3. Step 1: Maximum potential extent of forest cover 
To create the potential extent of standing forest cover, we combined the following layers with equal 
weights: ESA WorldCover 2021 tree cover and mangroves, WRI Tropical Tree Cover 2020 (≥70% 
tree cover), and Global Mangrove Watch (GMW) 2020. We excluded tree cover from the UMD Global 
Land Cover and Land Use 2019 dataset in Step 1 due to its coarser resolution and redundancy with 
other global tree cover products. We incorporated the Copernicus LCFM 2020 tree cover and 
mangrove dataset only where it coincided with tree cover or mangroves identified in ESA 
WorldCover 2020 or 2021 with at least two out of 3 layers indicating tree cover. We used the 
Google Natural Forest layer in three ways. First, we included areas with a forest probability ≥40% 
that filled gaps in WRI Tropical Tree Cover 2020 to mitigate visual artefacts at dataset boundaries 
and potential discontinuities due to tiling. Second, we included areas with a forest probability ≥70% 
where ESA WorldCover 2021 classified land as shrubland or grassland within the following FAO 
Global Ecological Zones: Tropical Moist Deciduous Forest, Tropical Dry Forest, Tropical Mountain 
System, Subtropical Mountain System, and Temperate Mountain System. Third, to reduce potential 
overestimation in dry tropical domains, we excluded areas with <60% probability in the Google 
Natural Forest layer that were also mapped as shrubland in Copernicus LCFM with <20% tree cover 
(Copernicus TCD), and where WRI Drivers indicated no disturbance or only non-permanent 
disturbance, from Step 1.  
To delineate the potential extent of temporarily unstocked forest, we first identified areas not 
included in the potential standing forest extent (see previous paragraph) where UMD data indicated 
>10% tree cover density in 2000, followed by tree cover loss between 2001 and 2020, and where 
the dominant driver was classified as non-permanent disturbance in the WRI Drivers dataset (see 
Table 1 for more details). We applied the same approach to: (i) areas with >10% tree cover density 
in 2010 from UMD Tree Cover 2010 and tree cover loss from non-permanent disturbance between 
2011 and 2020 from UMD Global Forest Cover Loss and WRI Drivers datasets; (ii) areas with >10% 
tree cover density in 2000 that were mapped as burned forest in the UMD Global Forest Cover Loss 
from Fire dataset (2001–2020); and (iii) areas mapped as plantation forest in the IIASA Forest 
Management dataset where recent tree cover loss (2011–2020) was detected. For planted forests 
in the IIASA Forest Management dataset with rotation periods longer than 15 years, we applied 
stricter criteria: we included only areas with >10% tree cover density in 2010 and canopy height >5 
m in 2020, based on the GSCH dataset. All rules combining historical tree cover, tree cover loss, and 
specific disturbance drivers or forest management types were spatially constrained to areas 
classified as shrubland or grassland in WorldCover 2021. We further excluded areas where post-
loss land use was identified as pastureland in WRI Global Pasture Watch. 
We combined the potential standing forest cover and the potential temporarily unstocked forest to 
produce the Step 1 output, which represents the maximum potential extent of forest cover. In 
parallel, we applied a conservative approach to delineate high confidence areas of natural and 
planted forest, which serve as safeguards to prevent excessive masking in Step 2. 
We defined high confidence natural forest by overlaying areas that IIASA Forest Management 
classified as naturally regenerating, with or without signs of management, and where the Google 
Natural Forest layer indicated a forest probability of at least 80%. We also included areas identified 
as mangroves in Global Mangrove Watch, undisturbed tropical moist forest up to 2020 in the JRC 
TMF dataset, and areas where WRI Drivers indicated no disturbance or only natural disturbance and 
IIASA classified the forest as naturally regenerating without signs of management. We excluded 
areas from this mask where IIASA Forest Management identified rubber plantations, where UMD 
 
19 
detected tree cover loss, or where JRC TMF detected disturbance (deforestation or forest 
degradation) up to 2020 in areas dominated by permanent disturbance according to WRI Drivers.  
We defined high confidence planted forest by first using the extent of planted forest from WRI 
SDPT version 3.0. We excluded areas with less than 10% tree cover in both UMD datasets for 2000 
and 2010, areas that IIASA Forest Management classified as rubber plantations, and areas that JRC 
TMF identified as agricultural tree plantations, mainly rubber and oil palm. These exclusions ensure 
that the SDPT dataset complies with the definition of planted forest under the EUDR. In addition, we 
expanded the high confidence planted forest mask by including areas that IIASA Forest 
Management mapped as planted or plantation forest where the probability of logging in WRI Drivers 
exceeded 85%. We also included areas identified as potentially temporarily unstocked forest in Step 
1 where the probability of logging in WRI Drivers exceeded 80%.  
2.1.4. Step 2: Exclusion of areas outside forest definition 
Three exclusion masks (tree height, agriculture use and other land use) were applied to the 
maximum potential extent of forest cover (from Step 1) to align to the forest definition. 
• Tree height: We excluded areas with top canopy heights below 5 meters when both the 
UMD GFCH and GSCH gridded products (Pauls et al., 2024) reported heights under this 
threshold. However, we retained areas where the JRC TMF dataset identified any type of 
tropical moist forest, including undisturbed, degraded, regrowing, or mangrove forests, as 
well as areas overlapping with high confidence natural or planted forest defined in Step 1. 
We did not apply this mask to Boreal coniferous forest, Boreal tundra woodland, Boreal 
mountain system, or Polar ecological zones. In addition, we excluded areas where at least 
two of the three canopy height products UMD GFCH, GSCH, and META WRI reported canopy 
heights below 5 meters under the following conditions: First, we applied this rule in areas 
mapped as primary or naturally regenerating forest in the IIASA Forest Management 
dataset where WRI Drivers identified logging as the dominant driver with a probability 
below 50 percent, as this situation may indicate an overestimation of unstocked forest area 
in Step 1. Second, we applied the rule in areas where WRI Drivers indicated no disturbance 
or only wildfire or natural disturbances, where at least one data source classified the land 
cover as non-tree cover according to ESA WorldCover 2020 or 2021 or Copernicus LCFM 
2020, and where Google Natural Forest indicated a forest probability of 50 percent or 
lower. We applied this condition to reduce potential overestimation of natural standing 
forest cover in Step 1. We made an exception for areas identified as high confidence natural 
or planted forest.  
• Agricultural use: We generated this mask by integrating multiple datasets on recent 
deforestation and cropland, and we gave additional emphasis to identifying tree crops, 
shifting agriculture, and agroforestry systems.  
o Deforestation: We used tree cover loss data from 2001 to 2020 produced by 
Hansen et al., 2013 in areas dominated by commodity driven deforestation, shifting 
agriculture, hard commodities, and settlements and infrastructure as identified by 
Sims et al., 2025. We excluded from the mask areas that overlapped with forest 
cover loss due to fire reported by Tyukavina et al., 2022, as well as areas classified 
as high confidence planted forest, JRC TMF forest including undisturbed, degraded, 
and regrowth classes, and mangroves reported by Vancutsem et al., 2021. These 
areas retain potential for recovery or regeneration, and tree cover loss in these 
 
20 
contexts does not necessarily indicate a change in land use. We also added areas of 
deforestation with or without subsequent regrowth from JRC TMF, considering only 
regrowth younger than five years. This category included deforestation to 
agricultural plantations (mainly rubber and oil palm) identified by JRC TMF, except in 
areas classified as high confidence natural or planted forest. We excluded areas 
where forest regrowth exceeded five years because we assumed that forest 
remained the dominant land use following regeneration on abandoned agricultural 
land. We included regrowth younger than five years because these areas may not 
meet the forest definition in terms of land use, tree cover, or height, for example in 
shifting agriculture systems. 
o Cropland: We produced a maximum extent mask of global cropland from UMD for 
the period 2003 to 2019 based on Potapov et al., 2022. We also incorporated the 
extent of World Cereal temporary crops and UMD GLC cropland, except in areas 
identified as high confidence natural or planted forest. In addition, we fully 
integrated ESA WorldCover cropland for the year 2021 into this exclusion mask. 
Within the tropical rainforest ecological zone, we prioritized excluding cropland data 
from GCEP where it overlapped with areas showing canopy heights below 5 meters 
according to META WRI and GSCH.   
o Tree crops: We produced a maximum extent of tree crops from the WRI SDPT 
dataset merging V1.0, V2.1 and V3.0 together, excluding areas identified as high 
confidence natural or planted forest. We extended this mask to areas mapped as 
fruit trees in the IIASA Forest Management dataset, except where they overlap with 
≥60% natural forest probability in the Google product or with high-confidence 
planted forest, and to areas mapped as palm, coffee, rubber, and cocoa based on a 
combination of probability and stratification layers. For palm plantation, we included 
the extent of global industrial and smallholder oil palm and coconut plantation from 
Descals et al., 2021, 2023 and 2024, together with the palm probability dataset 
from Google, except in areas mapped as mangroves in GMW or high confidence 
planted forest (more details on the probability threshold in Table 1). For coffee 
plantations, we used the Google coffee probability dataset. However, we excluded 
areas where: (i) coffee crop production was less than 200 ha according to the 
CROPGRIDS dataset; (ii) land was mapped as high-confidence planted forest or 
naturally regenerating forest without signs of management (i.e., primary forest) in 
the IIASA Forest Management dataset; (iii) the Google natural forest probability 
exceeded 80%; or (iv) the JRC TMF dataset classified the area as undisturbed moist 
forest. For rubber plantations, we used the Google rubber probability dataset, except 
in areas mapped as high-confidence natural forest or primary forest in the IIASA 
Forest Management dataset, or where UMD detected tree cover loss due to fire 
between 2001 and 2020 (as rubber is often misclassified as burned forest). For 
cocoa plantation, we used the Google cocoa probability dataset, except in areas 
cocoa crop production was less than 50 ha according to the CROPGRIDS dataset or 
where IIASA Forest Management mapped the land as naturally regenerating and the 
Google natural forest probability exceeded 80%. Finally, we used coffee, cocoa and 
cashew crop area from CROPGRID as stratification layer to refine the masking of 
tree crops with specific rules based on canopy height (less than 5m from META/WRI 
or GSCH products), cropland (i.e. GCEP) or potential agricultural commodity datasets 
(i.e. other land cover classification from JRC TMF) except in areas mapped as high 
confidence natural or planted forests.

o Shifting agriculture: We identified areas as shifting agriculture where shifting 
cultivation had a ≥60% probability and was the dominant driver of forest loss in the 
WRI Drivers dataset, and where areas were mapped as naturally regenerating forest 
in the IIASA Forest Management dataset, had canopy height <5 m in the META/WRI 
dataset, and were classified as degraded, deforested, or forest regrowth in 2020 in 
the JRC TMF dataset, excluding areas mapped as tree cover loss due to fire (2001–

• Other land use: We generated this mask by integrating multiple datasets on non-forest

artefacts. 
o Non-forest: We identified areas as non-forest ecosystems where the Google natural

dataset), and where at least two of the three canopy height products (UMD GFCH, 
GSCH, and META/WRI) reported canopy heights <5 m; we did not apply these rules in 
areas mapped as high-confidence natural or planted forest. We also applied a water 
mask derived from the JRC Global Surface Water database, including permanent 
water, new permanent water, seasonal-to-permanent water transitions, and 
permanent water bodies from the 2021 ESA WorldCover dataset. In addition, we 
used the “other land cover” class from the UMD dataset, covering true desert, semi-
arid areas, dense short vegetation, salt pans, sparse wetland vegetation, and ice, as 
an exclusion layer, except where it overlapped with tree cover loss due to fire (UMD) 
or high-confidence planted forest. We further applied wetland and bare soil classes 
from the 2020 and 2021 ESA WorldCover datasets, as well as bare soil from 
Copernicus LCFM 2020, as exclusion layers, except in areas mapped as high-
confidence planted forest. Finally, we incorporated the IIASA mining dataset to mask 
trees under mining land use where coinciding with tree cover loss from UMD, except 
in areas mapped as high-confidence planted forest. 
o Urban use: we took the maximum extent of UMD GLC built-up (0-100% built-up 
classes), JRC GHS-BUILT-C and built-up classifications from WorldCover (2020, 
2021), Copernicus LCFM 2020 and ESRI LULC 2020 to create an urban mask. We 
also included 0-10% built up class from UMD GLC where META/WRI indicated less 
than 5m height. We included JRC GHS SMOD to mask out trees under urban use 
(pre-processing details in Table 1).  
o Artefacts remaining in input datasets that propagated to the final map such as the 
regionally visible stripe patterns due to the Landsat-7 scan line corrector failure 
were manually masked out or filled using World Cover Tree Cover 2021 extent. The 
mask from the Global Surface Water database on lava flows was used to remove 
mapped forest pixels in volcanic area. Finally, isolated pixels forming blocky

2.1.5. Step 3: Post-processing 
To align with the forest definition, we applied a minimum mapping unit of 0.5 hectares. Patches of 
forest that did not reach the area of 0.5ha were labelled non-forest. Likewise, non-forest patches 
smaller than 0.5ha were labelled forest. This post-processing operation was based on the 
computation of each pixel area in square meters (latitude-dependent). We used the eight-neighbour 
rule to map patches using connected pixels in cardinal and intercardinal directions.  
2.2. Mapping approach for GFT2020 version 1 
2.2.1. Input datasets 
Implementing forest type definitions at the global scale, particularly the distinction between 
primary, naturally regenerating, planted, and plantation forests, remains constrained by the limited 
availability of consistent and publicly accessible global datasets. Mapping primary forests presents 
a major challenge because there are no universally agreed spatial indicators or methodologies that 
reflect the complexity of the definition. For this class we identify areas with minimal human 
disturbance and limited pressure on ecological processes. To that end we combine maps of large 
and continuous forest patches with data layers that indicate highly protected areas alongside 20–
30-year time series capturing human-induced forest disturbances (see Table 2). For mapping 
planted and plantation forests, we integrate a globally harmonized compilation of national planted 
forest datasets with consistent global remote sensing products that capture forest management 
type, tree cover, tree height, and tree cover loss, along with their respective direct drivers. 
Under the EUDR definition, forest degradation refers to the conversion of primary forest to 
plantation forest, planted forest, or other wooded land, or the conversion of naturally regenerating 
forest to plantation forest or other wooded land (see Annex 1). Therefore, the classification of the 
initial state in 2020 only requires a distinction between three categories: primary forest, naturally 
regenerating forest, and other forest types, which by definition include planted and plantation 
forests. In this context, GFT2020 groups planted and plantation forests into a single category. From 
a remote sensing perspective, mapping planted forest presents greater challenges than mapping 
plantation forest. Planted forests often have longer rotation periods, less regular spacing, and more 
heterogeneous age structures. At maturity, they can resemble naturally regenerating forests in both 
structure and composition. Plantation forests, in contrast, typically consist of one or two species, 
even age classes, and regular spacing, which produce more uniform and clearly detectable spectral 
and textural patterns in satellite imagery. 
The JRC released a preliminary version of GFT2020 (version 0) in December 2024. This map built on 
forest cover mapped in GFC2020 V1. The prototype map, produced with a simplified approach, 
served the collection of initial feedback. In contrast, the production of GFT2020 V1 is based on the 
latest version of GFC2020 (version 3) and employs more input data layers and a significantly 
elaborated methodology. We therefore do not report this methodology as an update of the 
preliminary map, and we also do not show statistical comparisons between GFT2020 V0 
(preliminary map) and V1 (the map of this report).

2.2.2. Approach 
The approach for mapping forest types in year 2020 at 10m resolution builds on the extent of 
forest mapped in GFC2020 V3, which by default is considered naturally regenerating forest. There 
are three main steps:  
• Step 1: We map the extent of planted and plantation forests by combining global collections 
of national and regional data on planted forests with a wall-to-wall forest management 
map stratified by drivers of forest loss and levels of forest naturalness. We also include 
areas of currently unstocked forest land resulting from harvesting operations. We apply a 
Minimum Mapping Unit (MMU) of 0.5 ha to the resulting planted/plantation forest.  
• Step 2: We map the extent of primary forests by creating a maximum primary forest layer 
that combines datasets on intact forests, high-integrity landscapes, and protected areas. We 
then exclude areas where anthropogenic disturbances have been recorded over the past 
20–30 years, as well as areas identified as planted or plantation forests in Step 1. To 
further prevent overlap between primary forest and anthropogenic areas, we removed tree 
crop and mining classifications (sourced from WRI SDPT V3 and IIASA) from the primary 
forest extent. While GFC2020 V3 limited the use of these datasets due to uncertainty and 
resolution constraints, we apply here a strict exclusion rule to ensure the integrity of the 
primary forest extent. Finally, we apply a MMU of 3 ha to the resulting primary forest. 
• Step 3: We assemble the outputs of Steps 1 and 2 within the forest cover extent of 
GFC2020 V3. In doing so, we reclassify planted forest patches smaller than 0.5ha (MMU at 
the end of Step 1) and primary forest patches smaller than 3 hectares (MMU at the end of 
Step 2) as naturally regenerating forest. We then address isolated forest patches by 
checking whether mapped primary or planted forest areas fall below 0.5 hectares; if they 
do, the algorithm assigns naturally regenerating forest. In the final step, we relabel 
naturally regenerating forest patches smaller than 0.5 hectares to match the dominant 
surrounding class (primary or planted forest). We make exceptions for gaps caused by 
selective logging (such as roads, skid trails, and log decks), shifting cultivation, or fire in 
tropical regions, and we keep these areas classified as naturally regenerating forest even 
when they are smaller than 0.5 hectares. 
Figure 2 illustrates the steps and decision rules in detail. We design the selection and integration of 
input datasets to identify planted or plantation forests with relatively high confidence. When 
multiple data layers lack supporting evidence or present contradictions, we classify the area as 
naturally regenerating forest. Similarly, for primary forest, we adopt a conservative approach, 
classifying a forest as primary only if it meets strict criteria for intactness, low fragmentation or 
protected status, and if all such areas show no evidence of human disturbance.

2.2.4. Step 2: Extent of primary forest 
To create the extent of primary forest, we first identified potential primary forest using the Forest 
Landscape Integrity Index and applied the pre-processing steps described in Table 2 for this layer. 
We then incorporated the Intact Forest Landscape dataset, undisturbed forests (including 
mangroves) from JRC TMF, and protected areas from the WDPA and EPFD. In boreal forest 
ecological zones, as defined by FAO, we added areas mapped as tree cover loss from fire (2001–
2020) in UMD Global Forest Change, areas where wildfire was the dominant driver in WRI Drivers of 
Tree Cover Loss, areas classified as primary forest in IIASA Forest Management Map, and areas 
where the Google probability of natural forest was ≥60%. 
From this potential primary forest extent, we excluded areas where the dominant driver was 
anthropogenic in WRI Drivers of Tree Cover Loss, or wildfire in tropical moist forests from JRC TMF, 
assuming that all fires in this domain are directly or indirectly human-induced, and where UMD tree 
cover loss, JRC TMF deforestation or degradation, or RADD disturbance alerts occurred up to the end 
of 2020. Additionally, we removed areas already classified as planted/plantation forest from Step 1, 
tree crops from SDPT V3, and mining areas from the IIASA dataset. Finally, we applied a minimum 
mapping unit (MMU) of 3 ha to generate the final primary forest extent. 
2.2.5. Step 3: Post-processing 
Post-processing in Steps 1 and 2 removed planted forest patches smaller than 0.5 ha (Figure 3A) 
and primary forest patches smaller than 3 ha (Figure 3B), respectively. In Step 3, we cross these 
two inputs with JRC Global Forest Cover V3 (GFC2020 V3) to reclassify those removed patches as 
naturally regenerating forest. We then applied multiple MMU cleanings to reduce spurious 
fragmentation in the three-class forest-type product, which reconciles the baseline GFC2020 V3 
with thematic masks for primary and planted/plantation forest. The baseline GFC2020 defines the 
forest domain and assigns the default class “naturally regenerating forest” wherever forest exists 
and no override is needed. The two auxiliary masks act as constraints, enforcing the “primary” class 
and the “planted” class, respectively. MMU logic prevents small, isolated patches of naturally 
regenerating forest from persisting within or along the edges of primary or planted areas, avoiding 
a salt-and-pepper pattern inconsistent with the intended cartographic resolution. 
The workflow first reclassified all overlapping pixels of naturally regenerating forest from GFC2020 
V3 with the primary forest mask as primary forest. It then evaluates the spatial contiguity of 
remaining naturally regenerating pixels using 8-neighbour connected-component analysis. For each 
pixel, we calculate the connected component’s size in area; components ≤5,000 m² (0.5 ha) fall 
below the MMU. Naturally regenerating pixels within these small components are reclassified to 
primary, producing an MMU-corrected “primary-enforced” map (Figure 3C). The procedure is 
repeated independently for the planted forest: pixels flagged by the planted/plantation mask are set 
to planted/plantation and the remaining naturally regenerating pixels are analyzed for connected-
component size, and components ≤0.5 ha are reclassified to planted/plantation, yielding an MMU-
corrected “Planted-enforced” map (Figure 3D). 
We then merged the two corrected products into a single categorical map giving priority to 
planted/plantation forest followed by primary forest and finally naturally regenerating forest. Pixels 
outside the forest domain remain mapped as non-forest. 
Finally, we applied a last consistency check to the merged map to remove any residual naturally 
regenerating fragments below 0.5 ha MMU. We isolated naturally regenerating pixels, recomputed 
connected-component areas using 8-neighbour connectivity and the 0.5 ha threshold, and 
 
29 
reclassified the given pixels or below the MMU to the dominant surrounding forest type (i.e., primary 
or planted/plantation). 
Overall, the MMU procedure acts as an area-based cartographic generalization. It preserves the 
three-class logic (primary, planted/plantation, naturally regenerating within the forest domain) while 
enforcing minimum patch sizes for naturally regenerating forest and reducing micro-patches 
caused by mask interactions or pixel-level noise. Exceptions include gaps within primary forest 
patches caused by human-driven disturbances in the tropics, such as selective logging (roads, skid 
trails, log decks), shifting cultivation, or tropical fires, identified using datasets such as forest 
degradation from the JRC TMF, logging areas from the WRI Drivers of Tree Cover Loss, or 
disturbance alerts from RADD. These areas, often smaller than 0.5 ha, remain classified as naturally 
regenerating forest.

2.3. Accuracy assessment of GFC2020 V3 
We conducted an accuracy assessment of the GFC2020 V3 map with an independent validation 
dataset to evaluate the accuracy of the map at the global and continental levels. The validation is 
done following good practices for accuracy assessment of land cover maps (Strahler et al., 2006) 
and meets the requirements of stage 3 validation guidelines of the Land Product Validation (LPV) 
subgroup of the Committee on Earth Observing Satellites (CEOS) (Tyukavina et al., 2025). The full 
protocol, including sampling design, response design, and statistical analysis, is described in detail 
and was applied to estimate the accuracy of GFC2020 V2 in Colditz et al. (2025a). The following 
section outlines the main steps of this protocol and its application to GFC2020 V3. 
The accuracy assessment uses 21,752 sample units from the validation dataset of the Copernicus 
Global Land Service Land Cover product for the year 2015 (CGLS-LC100) (Tsendbazar et al., 2020, 
2021) that consist of Primary Sample Units (PSU) of 100x100m divided into 100 10x10m 
Secondary Sampling Units (SSU). A protocol (Colditz et al., 2025a) was developed to reinterpret and 
label sample units using very high resolution (VHR) imagery near the EUDR cut-off date (31 
December 2020), applying the EUDR forest definition to the central 10×10 m SSU while considering 
the broader PSU context to interpret land-use and assess patch size and connectivity. Interpreters 
also reported on the forest type or the land use type for non-forest assignments.  
From the 21,752 sample units, interpreters could not assign classes to 24 sample units while 62 
sample units were not associated with a strata class. For the comparison of forest area estimates 
with FAO-FRA 2025, we intersected the sample with the FAO Global Assessment Unit Layer (FAO 
2015), reducing the sample by an additional 54 sample units. The final reference set (Colditz et al., 
2025b) with 21,612 sample units was crossed with GFC2020 V3 to report the accuracy metrics. We 
use the formulas from Stehman (2014), applicable to the case where the stratification does not 
correspond to the map and to account for unequal inclusion probabilities of sampled units. From 
this probability confusion matrix we derived overall accuracy, producer and user accuracy and 
associated omission and commission errors. We derived forest area and variance estimates 
(reported for the 95% confidence interval) from the final reference set following the stratified 
estimation approach from Stehman (2013) and Stehman (2014).  
We then compared area estimates from the reference dataset at global and continental scales with 
those derived from the three versions of the GFC2020 map (V1, V2, and V3), as well as with global 
and regional estimates reported in FAO-FRA-2025 (FAO, 2025) for 2020 and the FAO FRA-2020 
Remote Sensing Survey for 2018, which was based on a sample of over 400,000 units (FAO, 2022). 
Forest areas derived from GFC2020 are calculated by summing the surface area of all pixels 
mapped as forest within the GAUL country delineation (FAO 2015) in a WGS84-referenced 
geographic coordinate system; the geodesic area calculation inherently accounts for latitude-
dependent pixel size. The global forest area from FAO-FRA builds on national reporting of forest 
area for year 2020 reported in the FRA-2025. 
Note that no formal accuracy assessment for the three main forest types in GFT2020 is yet 
available; it is only applicable to the forest/non-forest mapping.

3.2. The global forest cover map for year 2020 V3 
3.2.1. Map results 
The Global Forest Cover map for 2020 (V3) depicts the global forest extent according to the EUDR 
forest definition for that year (Figure 4). The highest forest proportions occur in boreal zones, 
including Canada and Siberia, as well as in the three tropical humid forest basins: the Amazon 
Basin, the Congo Basin, and insular South-East Asia (Figure 5A).  
Figure 4. Map of GFC2020 V3.  
 
Source: JRC. Country delineation from GISCO 2024. 
Spatial analysis of the relative difference between GFC2020 V2 and GFC2020 V3 shows a global 
decrease in forest cover in V3, with several distinct hotspots (Figure 5B and 5C). In South America, 
forest cover declines by more than 20% in the Caatinga and Cerrado biomes. Across Africa, 
deciduous forests and woodland savannas show similarly strong reductions. 
We also observe a 5–20% decrease in forest cover between map version 2 and 3 in boreal regions 
of Canada, the United States, and Sweden, as well as in the tundra–taiga transition zone. This 
reduction likely results from improved forest representation in open landscapes through the 
integration of tree cover density and height information. Additional notable decreases occur in West 
Africa, Central America, along the western margin of the Amazon Basin (including Peru, Ecuador, 
and Colombia), and in Indonesia (notably Java, Sumatra, and central Kalimantan). These declines 
likely reflect the exclusion of agricultural tree plantations, shifting cultivation, and agroforestry 
systems. 
Conversely, the analysis identifies localized hotspots of increased forest cover in GFC2020 V3 
compared to V2. These increases concentrate in the eastern Brazilian Amazon, Malaysia, and parts 
of Indonesia, reflecting improved representation of plantation forests, such as pulp plantations of 
acacia or eucalyptus against agricultural tree plantations (e.g. rubber and oil palm). In version 3 we 
also detect higher forest cover in the western and central United States, Canada, and scattered 
areas of Europe. These increases likely result from improved identification of temporarily unstocked 
forests following disturbance, enabled by the higher-resolution stratification layer (e.g., WRI Drivers) 
and refined decision rules.

The revised approach better captures unstocked forest areas following clear-cut harvesting (Figure 
6A) and fire (Figure 6B). It also clarifies the forest–non-forest boundary in dry tropical landscapes 
by applying an intermediate method that reduces the underestimation of forest in GFC2020 V1 
while avoiding the potential overestimation associated with other wooded land in GFC2020 V2 
(Figure 6C). In GFC2020 V3, shifting cultivation systems, characterized by mosaics of small 
cultivated fields and fallow land at different stages of vegetative recovery, are now excluded from 
forest cover (except when a forest regrowth signal of more than five consecutive years after 
deforestation observation is confirmed in the JRC-TMF product), compared to the partial exclusions 
applied in earlier versions (Figure 6D). Exclusions have also improved in mixed urban and 
agricultural landscapes. Agroforestry systems, which are difficult to detect and separate from 
natural forest, are now more effectively excluded through enhanced data inputs and refined 
decision rules (Figure 6E). However, this stricter approach may introduce new issues, such as more 
pronounced blocky patterns and reduced precision in forest edge delineation, partly driven by the 
100 m resolution of the IIASA Forest Management layer. For agricultural tree plantations, including 
both full-sun and shaded production systems, forest cover estimates now exclude these areas more 
accurately. This improvement results from incorporating additional datasets on tree crops, including 
coffee (Figure 6F) and cocoa plantations (Figure 6G).
